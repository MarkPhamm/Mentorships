# 3. Detach: the more desperate you are, the further you are from success

This is the hardest one, and the most important.

**Desperation is visible.** Recruiters, hiring managers and interviewers see it in your messages, hear it in your answers and feel it in how you negotiate. And it works against you every time: people want to hire someone who has options, not someone who needs to be rescued. The harder you grip, the more it slips away.

## What desperation looks like

- **Cold messages that beg:** "Please give me a chance, I will work very hard, I really need this opportunity."
- **Following up five times** in a week, or messaging every recruiter at the company at once.
- **Applying to everything** with the same resume, including jobs you don't want and aren't qualified for.
- **Over-explaining and apologizing** in interviews: "Sorry, I'm just a student, I don't have much experience, but…"
- **Selling yourself short:** agreeing to anything, accepting the first number, saying yes to roles you'll hate.
- **Taking every rejection personally,** then letting it leak into the next interview.

## What detachment looks like

Detachment doesn't mean not caring. It means **wanting it without needing it.** You care about the outcome, but your sense of worth doesn't depend on it.

| Desperate | Detached |
|---|---|
| "Please give me a chance, I really need this internship." | "I built a pipeline that scrapes 160K airline reviews into Snowflake. I'd love to hear how your team handles ingestion. Open to a 15-minute chat?" |
| "Sorry, I don't have much experience, but…" | "Here's what I've built, and here's what I'd want to learn on your team." |
| Accepts the first offer immediately. | "Thank you, I'm excited about this. Can we talk about the package?" |
| Rejection: "I'm not good enough." | Rejection: "What can I learn from this? Next hand." |

## How to build detachment

You can't fake detachment for long. You have to actually create it:

- **Have options.** One application in flight makes every email life or death. Ten in flight make each one just one hand. Build a pipeline, not a single bet.
- **Keep building.** Work on projects, write and learn because you'd do it anyway, not only to get hired. Someone who's busy getting better doesn't have time to be desperate, and has new things to talk about in every interview.
- **Separate your worth from the outcome.** A rejection is a decision about one role, one team, on one day. It isn't a verdict on you. Many rejections have nothing to do with you at all: the role closed, the budget changed, someone internal got it.
- **Focus on what you control.** You control the quality of your work, your profiles and your preparation. You don't control the hiring decision. Put your energy where it changes something.
- **Interview as an equal.** You're also deciding whether you want to work there. Ask real questions about the team, the stack and how they work. That isn't arrogance; it's what people with options do.
- **Know your walk-away point** for salary, role and conditions before you need it. You'll negotiate better, and you'll stop holding onto offers you'd regret.

## The paradox

The people who get the best offers are usually the ones who look like they don't need them. Not because they're pretending, but because they've built enough (skills, projects, options, a community) that they genuinely don't. Build that, and the desperation goes away on its own.

---

## Questions to ask yourself

- [ ] How many opportunities do I have in flight right now? If it's one or two, how do I get to ten?
- [ ] Would my last cold message read as confident or desperate?
- [ ] Do I know my walk-away point?
